    Module One

    What trading is — and what it is not

    Trading means buying or selling a financial instrument in an attempt to benefit from a change in its price or from a market-related outcome. A trader may try to profit from rising prices (a long position) or falling prices (a short position, where the product and account permit it). Investors often focus on long-term ownership and wealth accumulation; traders may focus on shorter-term price movements. The boundary is not absolute.

    Price, value, and expectations

    A market price is the price at which buyers and sellers are currently willing to transact. It is not a perfect statement of an asset's true value. Prices respond to new information, expectations, supply and demand, positioning, liquidity, and emotion. A good company can have a falling share price if expectations were even higher; a weak company can rally if results are less bad than feared.

    How a trade makes or loses money

    A simplified long trade buys first and sells later. If the sale price is higher than the purchase price, the difference is a gross gain. If it is lower, the difference is a gross loss. Actual results must also account for spreads, commissions, financing, exchange fees, taxes, and slippage.

    Gross P/L — simple long(Exit price − Entry price) × Quantity

    Example. Buy 10 shares at $50 and sell at $53. Gross profit = ($53 − $50) × 10 = $30. If total costs are $4, net profit before tax is $26. If the shares instead sell at $47, the gross loss is $30 before costs.

    Trading is probabilistic

    A setup is not a certainty. Even a strategy with a historical edge will have losing trades. The aim is not to be right every time; it is to make decisions where the average outcome is favourable after costs, while controlling the damage when wrong.

    Never confuse a lucky win with a good process. Judge decisions by whether they followed the plan — not by one outcome.

    Knowledge check

    1. In your own words, explain the difference between gross profit and net profit.
    2. Why can a trade with a correct market direction still lose money?
    3. Why is a single winning trade not proof that a strategy works?
    Module Two

    How the global financial system works

    Financial markets help businesses raise capital, governments borrow, savers invest, and participants transfer or hedge risk. Prices also communicate information about expectations and scarcity. Markets can be centralised exchanges, dealer networks, electronic platforms, or combinations of these.

    Primary versus secondary markets

    Primary market: a new security is issued — for example, a company sells shares in an initial public offering, or a government issues a new bond. Secondary market: existing securities trade between investors. Most everyday stock trading occurs here.

    Exchanges and over-the-counter (OTC) markets

    An exchange, such as a stock exchange or futures exchange, operates under formal rules and usually centralises order matching for listed instruments. OTC markets trade through dealer networks or platforms. Spot foreign exchange is largely OTC; there is no single global forex exchange and no single universal price. Quotes may differ by provider.

    Trading sessions and time zones

    Markets open according to local calendars, time zones, holidays, and product rules. Major FX activity often increases when London and New York sessions overlap, but activity varies by currency and news. Stocks generally trade during their exchange's regular hours, with some pre-market and after-hours trading. Crypto spot markets commonly operate continuously, but liquidity and prices still vary. Always check the specific venue's hours.

    Central banks, governments, and interest rates

    Central banks influence monetary conditions through policy rates, liquidity operations, and communication. Governments influence markets through spending, taxation, borrowing, and regulation. Inflation, employment, growth, fiscal deficits, and political events can affect currencies, bond yields, equity valuations, and commodities — but market reactions depend on expectations and context.

    A bond is a loan to an issuer. Bond prices and yields generally move in opposite directions: if an existing fixed-coupon bond's market price falls, its yield rises, all else equal. Higher expected interest rates can pressure some asset valuations, but relationships differ across countries, time horizons, and market conditions.

    The single most useful habitMarkets react to the difference between what happened and what participants expected — not just whether a headline sounds good or bad.

    Which market should a beginner choose?

    Do not choose a market because someone online claims it is the easiest way to get rich. Choose based on what you can understand, the legal products available in your country, the quality of the provider, the trading hours, the cost structure, and your ability to manage risk.

    • Shares or diversified funds may be more intuitive for learning long-term investing, though they can fall substantially.
    • Forex can look simple because it uses currency pairs, but leverage and OTC execution make it risky.
    • Futures and options have contract specifications and risk mechanics that require careful study.
    • Cryptoassets can trade continuously and move sharply; custody and platform risks matter as much as price risk.
    • Leveraged CFDs are complex products and may be restricted or unsuitable for retail clients in some jurisdictions.

    Investing, trading, and speculation

    Investing usually emphasises ownership, diversification, and long-term objectives. Trading emphasises repeatable decisions and shorter holding periods. Speculation takes risk in pursuit of a price move. All can lose money; the label does not make a product safe.

    Module Four

    Who participates, and why prices move

    Market participants

    Retail tradersIndividuals using personal accounts.
    Institutional investorsPension funds, insurers, mutual funds, asset managers.
    Banks & dealersQuote prices, facilitate transactions, hedge exposure, manage inventory.
    HedgersReduce an existing business or portfolio risk — e.g. an airline hedging fuel costs.
    SpeculatorsTake exposure because they expect a price move.
    Market makersQuote buy and sell prices under specific rules and conditions.

    Supply, demand, and order flow

    If aggressive buying consumes available sell orders, the market may move up to find additional sellers. If aggressive selling consumes buy orders, the market may move down. The impact depends on depth, order size, liquidity, and the venue. A large market order can move price more in a thin market than in a deep one.

    Liquidity and volatility are different

    Liquidity describes how easily you can trade without moving the price much; it is often reflected in tight spreads and available depth. Volatility describes how much and how quickly price changes. A market can be volatile but liquid, or quiet but illiquid. Both conditions matter for execution.

    News, expectations, and positioning

    A report can be positive in absolute terms but negative relative to expectations. Prices can also move before the public release as participants position themselves, then reverse when the details or guidance differ from consensus. No news event produces a guaranteed direction.

    Correlation and diversification

    Assets can move together or in opposite directions, and relationships can change during stress. Diversification spreads exposure across different risks; simply holding several assets that all depend on the same factor may not diversify much. Correlation is not fixed.

    Module Five

    Trading venues, brokers, and account setup

    What a broker does

    A broker or trading provider gives you access to products and execution, handles account records, and applies its own rules, fees, margin requirements, and withdrawal procedures. Depending on the product and jurisdiction, the provider may act as agent, principal/counterparty, or use other arrangements. Understand which applies.

    Due diligence checklist

    • Check the regulator's official register yourself; confirm the exact legal entity and website domain.
    • Read product disclosures, fee schedules, margin rules, execution policy, and client-money protections.
    • Confirm whether the product is an exchange-traded security, futures contract, CFD, forex product, or cryptoasset.
    • Understand deposit and withdrawal methods, processing times, conversion fees, and account restrictions.
    • Test the platform and customer support with a demo or small non-critical transaction before committing meaningful funds.
    • Use a unique password, multifactor authentication, device security, and withdrawal protections where available.
    • Never give anyone your seed phrase, private key, one-time code, or remote access to your device.

    Demo, cash, margin, and leveraged accounts

    A demo account lets you practise with simulated funds. It is useful for learning the interface and testing rules, but simulated fills may not reflect real liquidity, slippage, emotions, or execution. A cash account typically limits purchases to available funds under its rules. A margin account allows borrowing or leveraged exposure and may require additional collateral; losses can exceed the amount you expected to risk.

    Questions to answer before funding

    1. What exact legal entity holds my account?
    2. Who regulates it, and what protections apply in my country?
    3. Can I lose more than my initial deposit?
    4. How are spreads, commissions, financing, swaps, and conversion charges calculated?
    5. What happens during a market gap, outage, or margin call?
    6. How do I withdraw, and can the provider freeze or restrict transactions under its terms?
    RememberA professional-looking website, app-store listing, social-media following, or celebrity endorsement is not proof that a provider is safe or regulated.
    Module Six

    Orders, spreads, liquidity, and execution

    Bid, ask, and spread

    The bid is the price at which a buyer is currently willing to buy; the ask is the price at which a seller is currently willing to sell. The spread is ask minus bid. A trader usually buys at or near the ask and sells at or near the bid, so the spread is an immediate trading cost.

    SpreadSpread = Ask price − Bid price  ·  Example: bid 1.1000 and ask 1.1002 → spread 0.0002. A round trip may cost more once commissions, slippage, and financing are included.

    Common order types

    OrderWhat it doesImportant limitation
    MarketSeeks immediate execution at available prices.Price is not guaranteed; fast markets can create slippage.
    LimitBuys no higher than, or sells no lower than, a specified limit.May not fill, even if price briefly touches the limit.
    StopBecomes a market order when the trigger is reached.Execution price can be worse than the stop price in a gap or fast market.
    Stop-limitTrigger activates a limit order.Controls the limit price but may leave the position unfilled.
    Take-profitAttempts to close at a chosen profit level.Execution and trigger rules depend on provider/product.
    Trailing stopStop level follows price according to set rules.Can be triggered by ordinary volatility; gaps remain possible.

    Slippage and gaps

    Slippage is the difference between the expected and actual execution price. It may be favourable or unfavourable. A gap occurs when the next available price is far from the previous one, often after news, during low liquidity, or when a market reopens. A stop-loss is a risk-control instruction, not an absolute guarantee of the exact exit price.

    Leverage and margin

    Leverage provides exposure larger than the cash put up. For example, 10:1 leverage means $1,000 of margin may control $10,000 of exposure, subject to the provider's rules. A 1% adverse move on $10,000 exposure is $100 before costs — 10% of the $1,000 margin. A larger move can consume the margin and may cause forced liquidation or a negative balance depending on rules and protections.

    Approximate exposureExposure = Margin × Leverage

    Leverage magnifies gains and losses. It does not create an edge or make a weak strategy profitable.

    Module Seven

    Chart reading and technical analysis

    What a candlestick shows

    A candlestick summarises price movement during a chosen period. Its body shows the open-to-close range; its wicks (or shadows) show the high and low. A green/up candle commonly closes above its open, while a red/down candle closes below it, though platform colours can be changed. A candle is a record of past prices, not a prediction.

    Figure 7.1 — The parts of a candlestick. Body = open→close. Wicks = the extremes reached during the period.

    Timeframes

    A 1-minute chart shows short intervals; a daily chart shows one trading day per candle; a weekly chart summarises a week. Lower timeframes can contain more noise and costs relative to the size of a move. Higher timeframes may help show broader context. A timeframe should fit your strategy, schedule, and ability to monitor risk.

    Trend and market structure

    Uptrend: a sequence of rising swing highs and rising swing lows. Downtrend: a sequence of falling swing highs and falling swing lows. Range: price oscillates between broadly similar highs and lows.

    These descriptions are simplifications. Markets can transition between regimes, and different traders may mark swing points differently. Define your rules clearly so the same chart is not interpreted differently after the outcome is known.

    Indicators — what they can and cannot do

    • Moving averages smooth prices to help describe trend; they lag.
    • RSI measures recent momentum on a bounded scale; an "overbought" reading does not guarantee a fall.
    • MACD compares moving averages; signals can whipsaw.
    • ATR estimates recent volatility; it does not predict direction.
    • Volume can help interpret activity, but quality varies across venues.
    Five indicators that all use the same price data do not necessarily provide five independent confirmations. Never use an indicator as a substitute for position sizing or a stop plan.

    Two warnings before you turn the page.

    First: a pattern is not a signal. It is a shape that occasionally repeats. Thousands of charts have produced the same shape and then done something else entirely. Treat every pattern as a hypothesis, not an instruction.

    Second: every pattern in this section is drawn with an obvious, "textbook-perfect" outline. Real charts are messy. The double top that looks clean in a diagram will, in real life, have a lumpy first peak and a slightly higher second peak, and the support line will be crossed and re-crossed several times before the actual break. The skill is not memorising the shape — it is learning to define, in writing, the exact conditions under which you will act and the exact conditions under which you will admit you are wrong.

    Chart 01 · Reversal candle

    Hammer & Inverted Hammer

    Figure 1 — Both candles appear after a decline. The hammer is the more common; the inverted hammer needs confirmation.

    What it is

    A hammer is a single candle with a small body near the top and a long lower wick — usually at least twice the length of the body. It forms after a decline, at or near support. It is a picture of sellers pushing price down hard, then failing: buyers step in and lift price back to near the open.

    An inverted hammer has the same small body but the long wick points up. It looks like a shooting star but appears after a decline rather than a rally.

    How to read it

    • Context first. A hammer in the middle of a range means very little. A hammer at the bottom of a clean decline, at a level that has held before, means much more.
    • Look at the wick, not the colour. A hammer with a red body is still a hammer if the wick is long enough.
    • Volume confirms. Higher volume on the hammer suggests more participants rejected the low.

    Entry, stop, target — one reasonable reading

    EntryOn a break above the hammer's high, or on the close of the next candle if it closes above the hammer's body.
    StopBelow the hammer's low. If that low is broken, the rejection has failed.
    TargetPrior swing high, or a measured multiple of the risk (e.g. 2R).
    InvalidationA close below the hammer's low, or a slow drift sideways that takes three or four candles to make no progress.
    Common mistakeTreating every long-wick candle as a hammer. The pattern only becomes meaningful after a sustained move and at a defined level. A hammer in the middle of noise is just a candle.
    Chart 02 · Indecision candle

    Doji — standard, dragonfly, gravestone

    Figure 2 — All three carry the same message at the close: buyers and sellers ended the period roughly where they began.

    What it is

    A doji is a candle whose open and close are almost identical. The resulting body is a horizontal dash. It says: during this period, neither side won.

    The dragonfly has no upper wick — only a long lower one. It looks like a hammer but with an even smaller body. The gravestone has only a long upper wick; sellers completely rejected the high.

    How to read it

    • Location is everything. A doji at the top of an extended rally warns of exhaustion; at the bottom, of possible reversal; in the middle of a range, of nothing.
    • Doji do not predict direction. They predict uncertainty. The next candle's close decides which way the market resolves.
    • Weekly and daily doji carry more weight than 1-minute doji, which are extremely common.

    Entry, stop, target — one reasonable reading

    EntryDo not enter on the doji itself. Wait for the following candle to close above the doji's high (bullish) or below its low (bearish).
    StopBeyond the far side of the doji — below the low for longs, above the high for shorts.
    TargetNearest prior swing point or measured move equal to the prior impulse.
    InvalidationThe next candle closes back inside the doji's range and stays there.
    Doji remind us that most candles are noise. A single candle is never a trade; it is one sentence in a paragraph.
    Chart 03 · Two-candle reversal

    Bullish & Bearish Engulfing

    Figure 3 — A large candle completely covers the previous small candle's body. The size of the engulfing candle matters more than the colour.

    What it is

    An engulfing pair is two candles in which the second body completely covers the first. In a bullish engulfing, a green candle closes above the open of the previous red candle — often closing above its high. The reverse forms a bearish engulfing.

    The pattern is a direct picture of a change of control: one side was in charge for the first candle, and by the close of the second, the other side has taken over.

    How to read it

    • Size matters. A large engulfing candle carries more weight than a marginal one.
    • Levels matter. At the top of a trend, a bearish engulfing is more useful. In the middle of a range, it is often just noise.
    • Volume matters. A bullish engulfing that occurs on double the average volume is a much stronger observation.
    • The close matters. Weak engulfing that closes in the middle of the previous candle often fails.

    Entry, stop, target — one reasonable reading

    EntryAt the close of the engulfing candle, or on a break above/below its high/low.
    StopBeyond the extreme of the pair — below the low of the small candle for longs, above the high for shorts.
    TargetNext resistance/support, or a measured multiple of the candle's range.
    InvalidationA close back inside the range of the engulfing candle.
    Chart 04 · Rejection candle

    Shooting Star

    Figure 4 — A shooting star appears after a rally and at resistance. Buyers pushed price to the high, then sellers pushed it all the way back.

    What it is

    A shooting star has a small body near the low of the candle and a long upper wick — usually at least twice the body. It appears after an advance, often exactly at a resistance level. The message is that the last attempt to push higher failed, and the market gave back the gains before the close.

    How to read it

    • Location, again. At resistance after a strong rally, it is a warning. In the middle of a quiet range, it is a candle.
    • Colour is secondary. Green or red both work, though a red shooting star is generally read more strongly.
    • Follow-through is required. A shooting star that is immediately followed by another green candle that closes above its high has been ignored by the market — treat it as failed.

    Entry, stop, target — one reasonable reading

    EntryOn a break below the shooting star's low, or on the close of the next candle if it closes red.
    StopAbove the shooting star's high.
    TargetPrevious support, the mid-point of the prior impulse, or a fixed multiple of the risk.
    InvalidationA close back above the shooting star's body.
    Chart 05 · Three-candle reversal

    Morning Star & Evening Star

    Figure 5 — A large candle, a small "star" of indecision, then a large candle in the other direction. The star is the pivot.

    What it is

    A morning star is three candles at the bottom of a decline: a large red candle, then a small-bodied candle that gaps down or opens near the close of the first, then a large green candle that closes well into the body of the first red candle. The evening star is the mirror image at the top of a rally.

    How to read it

    • The star itself is the signal of pause. Its body should be small, and it should sit outside the range of the first candle.
    • Depth of the third candle matters. The more the third candle retraces into the first, the stronger the pattern.
    • Where it appears is what makes it useful. Morning stars at support after a long decline and evening stars at resistance after a long advance are the classic locations.

    Entry, stop, target — one reasonable reading

    EntryOn the close of the third candle, or on a break of the third candle's high (morning) / low (evening).
    StopBeyond the extreme of the star — below the star's low for morning, above the high for evening.
    TargetThe far end of the first candle's body, or a measured move equal to the range of the first candle.
    InvalidationA close back inside or beyond the first candle.
    Chart 06 · Reversal pattern

    Double Top — the "M"

    Figure 6 — Two attempts to break resistance, a failure at the second, then a decisive break below the neckline.

    What it is

    A double top is a reversal pattern: price rises to a level, pulls back, rises again to roughly the same level, and then falls. The two peaks look like the letter M. The horizontal line drawn through the middle dip is the neckline.

    How to read it

    • The two tops need not be identical. A second top slightly higher than the first is still commonly read as a double top — though a much higher second top is more likely to be a trend continuation.
    • The neckline is the trigger. Until the neckline breaks on a closing basis, nothing has actually happened.
    • Volume usually falls on the second top. If the second peak arrives on weaker volume than the first, the pattern carries more weight.
    • A failed double top is informative. If price breaks the neckline and then reclaims it, the bearish read is dead, and the reversal often turns into a squeeze higher.

    Entry, stop, target — one reasonable reading

    EntryOn the close below the neckline, or on a retest of the neckline from below that fails.
    StopAbove the second top, or — for a tighter stop — above the retest candle.
    TargetMeasured move: the height from the tops to the neckline, projected down from the breakdown.
    InvalidationA close back above the neckline, or a strong bullish candle that reclaims the second top.
    The M is only a pattern once the neckline breaks. Before that, it is a range with two failed breakouts — and it might resolve higher.
    Chart 07 · Reversal pattern

    Double Bottom — the "W"

    Figure 7 — Two failures to break support, then a rally through the neckline. The "W" is a reversal of a prior decline.

    What it is

    A double bottom is the mirror of a double top. Price falls to a support level, bounces, falls again to roughly the same level, and then rallies. The two lows form the letter W. The horizontal line drawn through the middle peak is the neckline.

    How to read it

    • The second bottom should not be dramatically lower than the first. A small undercut can actually be a positive sign — it sweeps stops and then reverses.
    • The rally through the neckline should be on rising volume. A weak, drifting breakout tends to fail.
    • Retests are common. After breaking the neckline, price often returns to it from above before continuing. A successful retest is a second, often better, entry.
    • Failure is clean and quick. If price closes back below the second bottom, the pattern is broken. There is no ambiguity to preserve.

    Entry, stop, target — one reasonable reading

    EntryOn the close above the neckline, or on the first successful retest of the neckline from above.
    StopBelow the second bottom, or below the low of the breakout candle for a tighter stop.
    TargetMeasured move: height of the pattern, projected up from the breakout.
    InvalidationA close back below the neckline, or a failure at the second bottom.
    Chart 08 · Reversal pattern

    Head & Shoulders

    Figure 8 — Three peaks: a lower left shoulder, a higher head, a lower right shoulder. The neckline joins the two intervening lows.

    What it is

    The head and shoulders is one of the oldest and best-known reversal patterns. Price makes three peaks: a first shoulder, a higher head, then a second shoulder that fails to reach the head. The two intervening lows define a neckline. When price closes below that neckline, the pattern is considered complete.

    How to read it

    • Symmetry helps but is not required. The right shoulder is often slightly smaller than the left.
    • The neckline is rarely perfectly horizontal. It slopes up or down, depending on the two intervening lows. A sloping neckline is still valid.
    • Volume typically falls across the pattern. Highest on the left shoulder's rally, lowest on the right.
    • False breaks are common. Price may dip just below the neckline and then reclaim it. This "bear trap" often produces a sharp move the other way.

    Entry, stop, target — one reasonable reading

    EntryOn the close below the neckline, or on a retest of the neckline from below.
    StopAbove the right shoulder (wider) or above the retest candle (tighter).
    TargetThe distance from the head to the neckline, projected down from the breakdown point.
    InvalidationA close back above the neckline, or a new high above the right shoulder.
    Chart 09 · Reversal pattern

    Inverse Head & Shoulders

    Figure 9 — The mirror of the head and shoulders. Three lows, the middle deepest. A break above the neckline completes the pattern.

    What it is

    The inverse head and shoulders is the upside-down version of the previous pattern: three troughs, with the middle one the lowest. It appears at the end of a decline and marks a shift from selling to buying. The neckline runs across the two intervening peaks, and a close above it completes the pattern.

    How to read it

    • The right shoulder often builds quietly. Volume tends to be lowest there before rising into the breakout.
    • The neckline break should be decisive. A close, not a wick.
    • Retests are common and useful. The first pullback to the neckline after the break is often the cleanest entry, because the stop is small and the pattern is fresh.
    • Failed inverse H&S — where price breaks the neckline, stalls, and rolls back down — are common in choppy, low-liquidity markets.

    Entry, stop, target — one reasonable reading

    EntryOn the close above the neckline, or on the first successful retest.
    StopBelow the right shoulder, or below the breakout candle.
    TargetHead-to-neckline height, projected up from the breakout.
    InvalidationA close back below the neckline, or a lower low beneath the right shoulder.
    Chart 10 · Continuation pattern

    Cup & Handle

    Figure 10 — A rounded decline, a rounded recovery to the old high, a small pause (handle), then a breakout to new highs.

    What it is

    The cup and handle is a bullish continuation pattern. After an advance, price pulls back in a rounded "U" (the cup), rallies back to the prior high, pauses in a shallow drift (the handle), and then breaks out above the old high. It is one of the few patterns explicitly designed for a market that is going up.

    How to read it

    • The cup should be a "U," not a "V." A sharp V-shape is usually a different pattern — often just a pullback within a trend.
    • The handle should be shallow and short. If it retraces more than about half the cup's depth, the pattern loses its meaning.
    • The handle often slopes down, but not always. A slightly rising handle is fine in strong trends.
    • Volume dries up in the handle. That quietness is part of the message: sellers are absent.
    • Breakout volume should expand sharply. A breakout on low volume is suspect.

    Entry, stop, target — one reasonable reading

    EntryOn the close above the rim (the high of the cup), ideally with above-average volume.
    StopBelow the handle's low, or below the breakout candle for a tighter stop.
    TargetMeasure the cup's depth (rim to bottom) and project it up from the breakout point.
    InvalidationA close back below the handle's low, or an obvious failure at the rim.
    Chart 11 · Continuation pattern

    Bullish Flag

    Figure 11 — A sharp advance (the pole), a tight downward-sloping channel (the flag), then a breakout above the flag.

    What it is

    A bullish flag is a brief pause inside a strong uptrend. Price makes a sharp, almost vertical advance (the pole), then drifts sideways-to-lower in a narrow parallel channel (the flag), and finally breaks out above the flag in the direction of the original move.

    How to read it

    • The pole should be strong and fast. Long, slow advances make for weak flags.
    • The flag should be tight. A wide, choppy pause is not a flag — it is a range.
    • Volume should contract in the flag and expand on the breakout.
    • Flags work on all timeframes — from 1-minute scalps to weekly charts.
    • Failed flags are informative. A break below the flag's lower boundary in a supposed uptrend often precedes a sharp reversal.

    Entry, stop, target — one reasonable reading

    EntryOn the close above the flag's upper boundary.
    StopBelow the flag's lower boundary, or below the last swing low inside the flag.
    TargetThe pole's height, projected up from the breakout.
    InvalidationA close below the flag's lower boundary.
    Chart 12 · Continuation pattern

    Pennant

    Figure 12 — Like a flag, but the pause is a small symmetrical triangle. The pole is the same, the target calculation is the same.

    What it is

    A pennant is almost identical to a flag, with one difference: instead of a parallel channel, the pause forms a small symmetrical triangle — two converging lines. It is read the same way, and traded the same way.

    How to read it

    • Pennants are usually shorter than flags. If the triangle drags on for weeks, it is no longer a pennant.
    • Breakouts often occur in the last third of the triangle. That is where the pattern is most "coiled."
    • Volume behaviour is the same: dry up inside, expand on the break.

    Entry, stop, target — one reasonable reading

    EntryOn the close above the pennant's upper line.
    StopBelow the pennant's lower line, or below its last swing low.
    TargetThe pole's height, projected from the breakout.
    InvalidationA close below the pennant's lower line.
    Chart 13 · Reversal pattern

    Falling Wedge (bullish)

    Figure 13 — Both boundary lines fall, but they converge. The selling pressure is weakening even though price is still drifting lower.

    What it is

    A falling wedge is a bullish pattern that appears at the end of a decline. Both the upper and lower boundaries slope downward, but they converge — the decline is slowing. When price breaks the upper line, the wedge is complete.

    How to read it

    • Not every falling channel is a wedge. The lines must converge, not stay parallel.
    • A rising wedge is the bearish mirror. Same convergence, opposite direction.
    • The apex is the decision point. Breakouts tend to occur before the lines meet.
    • Volume often falls through the wedge and rises on the break. The same behaviour as flags and pennants.

    Entry, stop, target — one reasonable reading

    EntryOn the close above the wedge's upper line.
    StopBelow the wedge's lower line, or below the last swing low inside the wedge.
    TargetThe widest part of the wedge (left edge), projected up from the breakout.
    InvalidationA close back inside the wedge, or a break below the lower line.
    Chart 14 · Continuation pattern

    Ascending Triangle

    Figure 14 — Buyers keep stepping in at higher prices, but sellers defend the same ceiling. When the ceiling finally breaks, price often runs.

    What it is

    An ascending triangle is a bullish pattern: a horizontal resistance line at the top and a rising support line below. Buyers are becoming more aggressive (higher lows), while sellers keep defending the same level. The squeeze usually resolves upward.

    How to read it

    • The rising support line should touch at least two higher lows. Three is better.
    • Volume often declines through the pattern. A breakout with weak volume is suspect.
    • Ascending triangles can also break down. If the rising line fails, the pattern fails. Define your invalidation in advance.
    • Rising wedges and ascending triangles are related but distinct. In a wedge both lines converge to a point; in an ascending triangle one line is flat.

    Entry, stop, target — one reasonable reading

    EntryOn the close above the horizontal resistance line.
    StopBelow the most recent higher low, or below the rising support line.
    TargetThe height of the triangle's base, projected up from the breakout.
    InvalidationA close below the rising support line.
    Chart 15 · Foundations

    Support & Resistance

    Figure 15 — Price touches the same areas repeatedly. Once a level breaks, it often flips its role: old resistance becomes new support.

    What it is

    Support is an area where buying has previously appeared; resistance is an area where selling has previously appeared. Neither is a precise price — both are zones. A level works because enough market participants remember it and act on it.

    How to read it

    • Draw zones, not lines. A support "level" is the range in which the previous lows clustered, not a single price.
    • The more touches, the stronger the level — up to a point. A level that has been tested five or six times often breaks dramatically, because the resting orders have already been absorbed.
    • Role reversal is common. When resistance breaks on a closing basis, it frequently becomes new support on the retest.
    • Volume and reaction matter. A touch with a sharp, immediate rejection is more meaningful than a touch that simply drifts through.
    • Context always matters. In a strong trend, levels are less reliable. In ranges, they are the whole game.

    Entry, stop, target — one reasonable reading

    EntryOn a confirmed rejection from the zone (a reversal candle closing away from it), or on a confirmed breakout and retest.
    StopBeyond the zone, plus a small buffer for wick noise.
    TargetThe opposite zone, or the next obvious swing point.
    InvalidationA decisive close through the zone, followed by a failed retest.
    Levels are not magic. They are areas where people previously made decisions. When the people change, the level changes.
    Chart 16 · Foundations

    Trendlines & Channels

    Figure 16 — An up-channel. Buy the lower line, sell the upper line — until the channel breaks.

    What it is

    A trendline is a straight line drawn through a series of higher lows (in an uptrend) or lower highs (in a downtrend). A channel is simply a pair of parallel trendlines. Channels describe the "road" a trending market is travelling along.

    How to read it

    • Two points make a line; three points make a trendline. A line drawn through only two points is provisional.
    • Do not force the line. If it requires ignoring two or three obvious wicks, it is the wrong line.
    • A trendline break is not a trend reversal. It is the first warning. Reversals require structure change — a lower low in an uptrend, a higher high in a downtrend.
    • Channels define expectations. In an up-channel, the lower line is where buyers appear; the upper line is where they tend to take profit.
    • Channels eventually break. The break is often violent, because both sides are wrong at the same time.

    Entry, stop, target — one reasonable reading

    EntryOn a confirmed bounce from the trendline (with a reversal candle), or on a decisive break of the trendline.
    StopBeyond the trendline, with a buffer.
    TargetThe opposite side of the channel, or the next structural level.
    InvalidationA close beyond the trendline, or a failure to make a new extreme in the direction of the trend.
    Chart 17 · Execution

    Breakout & Retest

    Figure 17 — The cleanest breakouts are often not the first candle through a level, but the moment price returns to test the old resistance as new support.

    What it is

    A breakout is a decisive close through a level — resistance, a trendline, the neckline of a pattern, or the rim of a cup. A retest is the moment price returns to that level from the other side, to confirm that it has changed role.

    How to read it

    • Wait for the close. A wick through a level is not a breakout. The close is what matters.
    • Prefer breakouts with expanding volume. A quiet breakout is often a trap.
    • Do not chase. If the first candle through the level is huge, the risk of entering at the top of the move is high. Wait for the retest.
    • A failed retest is a signal in the opposite direction. If price returns to the level and then breaks back through it in the original direction, the breakout was a fake, and the move in the opposite direction can be sharp.
    • Define your entry trigger in writing before the breakout happens. "Enter on a close above $X with volume > 1.5× the 20-bar average" is a rule. "Enter when it looks strong" is not.

    Entry, stop, target — one reasonable reading

    EntryOn the retest of the level from the other side, with a confirming candle closing in the direction of the breakout.
    StopOn the wrong side of the level — below the retest low for an upside break, above the retest high for a downside break.
    TargetThe height of the prior consolidation, the next major level, or a fixed R multiple.
    InvalidationA decisive close back through the level in the opposite direction.
    Module Eight

    Fundamental analysis and economic news

    Fundamental analysis by asset class

    StocksRevenue, profit margins, cash flow, debt, competitive position, management, valuation, future guidance.
    ForexRelative interest-rate expectations, inflation, growth, central-bank policy, trade flows, political risk.
    BondsInflation expectations, policy rates, issuer credit quality, duration, supply/demand.
    CommoditiesProduction, inventories, demand, weather, transport, geopolitics, futures-curve structure.
    CryptoassetsNetwork use, token supply/unlocks, security, governance, liquidity, regulation, concentration of holdings.

    Economic calendar

    An economic calendar lists scheduled releases such as inflation, employment, GDP, retail sales, purchasing-manager surveys, and central-bank decisions. It often shows a previous value, forecast/consensus, and actual value. The forecast is an estimate, not a promise. Check the definition, revision policy, release time, and the market's expectations.

    Company earnings

    For a company, compare reported results with prior periods and expectations. Look beyond headline earnings: revenue quality, margins, cash generation, debt, segment results, and management guidance may matter. A company can beat earnings but fall if guidance disappoints or the stock was already priced for stronger results.

    A simple news workflow

    1. Identify the event and the instrument most directly affected.
    2. Read a primary source where possible: central bank statement, official statistics release, company filing, or exchange notice.
    3. Record the consensus expectation and what would count as a meaningful surprise.
    4. Consider alternative interpretations and what the market may already have priced in.
    5. Check liquidity, spread, and gap risk before deciding whether trading the event is appropriate.
    6. If you cannot explain the risk and the invalidation point, do not trade simply because the news is dramatic.

    Being informed does not mean you can predict the immediate reaction. News trading can be especially difficult because spreads and volatility may jump.

    Module Nine

    Risk management and position sizing

    Why risk management comes first: a trader can be wrong repeatedly and still remain in the game if losses are controlled. A trader who risks too much can suffer a drawdown from which recovery becomes mathematically difficult.

    Risk per trade

    Many educational examples use a small fixed fraction of account equity — such as 0.5% or 1% — to illustrate risk budgeting. These are examples, not universally appropriate recommendations. Your suitable risk may be lower or zero depending on your finances, experience, product, and tolerance for loss.

    Risk budgetAccount equity × Risk percentage

    Example. Account equity is $2,000 and the planned risk is 0.5%. The risk budget is $10. That is the amount you intend to lose if the stop is executed at the assumed price; actual losses can be larger because of gaps, slippage, fees, and product mechanics.

    Position size

    Position size should be derived from the stop distance and the amount you are prepared to lose. The value per point, pip, tick, share, contract, or unit depends on the instrument and contract specification.

    Position sizeRisk budget ÷ (Stop distance × Value per price unit)

    Illustration. Risk budget = $10; stop distance = $2 per share. Ignoring fees and gaps, maximum quantity = $10 ÷ $2 = 5 shares. If the stop is $4 away, quantity would be 2.5 shares if fractional shares are supported. If only whole shares are allowed, round down to 2 shares.

    Risk-to-reward ratio

    If a setup risks $10 to target a possible $20, its planned reward-to-risk ratio is 2:1. That does not mean the target will be reached or that the trade is good. The setup's win rate, average win, average loss, trading costs, and execution quality determine results over many trades.

    Expectancy(Win rate × Average win) − (Loss rate × Average loss) − Average costs

    Example. Win rate 45%, average win $20, loss rate 55%, average loss $12, average costs $1 per trade. Expectancy = (0.45 × $20) − (0.55 × $12) − $1 = $0.40 per trade, on these assumptions. This is a hypothetical example, not a forecast.

    Drawdowns and recovery

    Loss from peakGain needed to recover to peak
    5%5.3%
    10%11.1%
    20%25.0%
    30%42.9%
    50%100.0%

    A 50% loss requires a 100% gain on the remaining capital to return to the starting amount. This is why preventing catastrophic losses matters.

    Practical risk rules

    • Know the maximum loss if the planned exit is executed, and acknowledge gap risk.
    • Set a daily/weekly loss limit and stop trading when it is reached.
    • Avoid increasing size to recover losses or moving a stop farther away merely to avoid taking a loss.
    • Account for correlated positions: several trades may be one large shared bet.
    • Avoid trading with rent, food, tuition, emergency savings, borrowed money, or money needed soon.
    • Never assume a stop order guarantees a precise exit price.
    • Review results over a meaningful sample, not a handful of trades.
    Module Ten

    Building and testing a trading strategy

    A strategy is a rule set

    A strategy should be specific enough that you can identify a valid setup before knowing the outcome. "Buy when it looks strong" is too vague. A testable strategy defines the market, timeframe, setup, entry trigger, stop, exit, position-sizing method, no-trade conditions, and review method.

    Strategy template

    ElementQuestions to answer
    Market / instrumentWhich exact product and venue? Trading hours and contract details?
    TimeframeWhich chart timeframe and holding period?
    ContextWhat trend, range, volatility, or fundamental conditions must be present?
    Entry triggerWhat objective event confirms entry?
    Invalidation / stopWhat price or condition shows the trade idea is wrong?
    ExitFixed target, trailing exit, time-based exit, or another written rule?
    Position sizeHow is size calculated from risk budget and stop distance?
    CostsHow are spread, commission, slippage, funding, and tax considered?
    No-trade rulesWhen do you stand aside — major news, poor liquidity, daily loss limit?
    EvidenceWhat historical and forward test would support or reject the idea?

    Backtesting

    Backtesting applies rules to historical data. Avoid look-ahead bias (using information unavailable at the time), survivorship bias (only testing assets that survived), and overfitting (tuning rules to match past noise). Include realistic costs and slippage assumptions. Keep some data separate for validation and avoid endlessly adjusting rules until the past looks perfect.

    Paper trading / forward testing

    Paper trading applies the rules to new market data without risking real money. Record every valid signal, including missed trades and losses. Use the same entry, stop, size, and exit rules you intend to use later. Demo success does not guarantee live success because fills, emotions, liquidity, and costs differ.

    Metrics to track

    • Win rate — winning trades ÷ total trades.
    • Average win / average loss — typical size of winners and losers.
    • Expectancy — average expected result per trade.
    • Maximum drawdown — largest peak-to-trough decline.
    • Profit factor — gross profits ÷ gross losses.
    • Rule adherence — percentage of trades that followed the written plan.

    A strategy with a high win rate can still lose money if losses are much larger than wins. A strategy with a lower win rate can be profitable if average wins sufficiently exceed average losses, after costs.

    Module Eleven

    Trading psychology and common mistakes

    The emotional cycle

    Fear can cause early exits; greed can cause oversized positions; hope can delay a necessary exit; frustration can trigger revenge trading. The answer is not to become emotionless. It is to design rules and routines that reduce the influence of emotion on decisions.

    Common mistakes

    OvertradingTaking low-quality setups because you feel you must be active.
    Revenge tradingIncreasing risk to win back a recent loss.
    FOMOEntering after a large move because you fear missing out.
    Moving stopsChanging risk rules mid-trade to avoid accepting a loss.
    OversizingUsing a position too large for your account and stop distance.
    Strategy hoppingAbandoning a system after a few losses without adequate evidence.
    Confirmation biasSearching only for information that supports your position.
    Cherry-pickingShowing winners and hiding losing trades.
    Ignoring costsAssuming gross backtest profits equal real net profits.
    Borrowed-money tradingAdding repayment pressure to an already uncertain activity.

    A pre-trade checklist

    1. Can I state the reason for the trade in one sentence?
    2. Does the setup match my written rules?
    3. Where is the invalidation point and planned exit?
    4. What is the cash amount at risk, including estimated costs?
    5. Is this position correlated with trades I already hold?
    6. Is there a scheduled event or liquidity condition that changes the risk?
    7. Am I calm enough to accept the planned loss?
    8. If this trade loses, will I still be within my daily and overall limits?

    Post-trade review

    Record whether you followed the plan, not just whether you won. A planned loss can be a good trade; an impulsive win can reinforce dangerous behaviour. Review patterns across many trades and change rules only through a documented process.

    Module Twelve

    Scams, safety, taxes, and regulation

    Warning signs

    • Guaranteed daily or monthly returns, risk-free profits, or claims of a secret method.
    • Pressure to deposit immediately, pay a fee to unlock withdrawals, or recruit others.
    • Unsolicited messages from supposed account managers, signal sellers, or recovery agents.
    • A provider that is absent from the relevant regulator's register or impersonates a legitimate firm's name.
    • Requests for your password, one-time code, wallet seed phrase, private key, or remote device access.
    • Screenshots of profits with no independently verifiable statement or risk history.
    • A demand to pay taxes or withdrawal fees to a personal wallet before funds can be released.

    Protect your accounts and cryptoassets

    Use unique passwords and multifactor authentication; protect the email account tied to the trading account. Bookmark official sites instead of following unsolicited links. Verify wallet addresses and networks carefully; crypto transfers are often irreversible. Never share seed phrases or private keys. Legitimate support should not need them. Understand custody: who controls the assets, keys, or client money, and what happens if the provider fails?

    Regulation

    Financial rules differ by country and by product. A firm authorised for one service may not be authorised for another. Verify the exact legal entity and permission using the regulator's official website, not a link supplied only by the firm. Check local rules in your country of residence and where the provider operates. This notebook does not establish that any particular broker or product is legal or suitable for you.

    Tax and records

    Trading may create taxable income, capital gains, reporting duties, or other obligations depending on the instrument and jurisdiction. Keep statements, trade confirmations, funding/withdrawal records, fees, exchange rates, and calculations. Consult official tax guidance or a qualified local professional; do not assume that offshore or crypto activity is tax-free.

    If you have already sent money to a suspected scamStop sending additional funds. Preserve messages and transaction records. Contact your bank or platform promptly. Report it to the relevant authorities. Be wary of recovery scams that promise to retrieve funds for an upfront payment.

    Review

    Twelve questions, with answers

    1. What is the difference between a primary and secondary market?
      Primary markets issue new securities; secondary markets trade existing securities.
    2. Why can a market order receive a worse price than expected?
      Available prices can change before execution, especially in fast or illiquid markets; this is slippage.
    3. What does leverage do?
      It increases exposure relative to the margin/capital committed, magnifying gains and losses.
    4. Does a stop-loss guarantee the exact exit price?
      No. Gaps and fast markets can lead to worse execution; product rules matter.
    5. What is the spread?
      The difference between the ask and bid prices.
    6. Why might positive company news cause its share price to fall?
      The result may disappoint relative to expectations, guidance may be weak, or the news may already be priced in.
    7. How do you calculate a simple risk budget?
      Account equity multiplied by the chosen risk percentage.
    8. What is expectancy?
      A measure of average outcome per trade based on win rate, average wins/losses, and costs.
    9. Why is a backtest not proof of future profits?
      Historical data may not represent future conditions; biases, overfitting, costs, and changing regimes can distort results.
    10. Name three signs of a trading scam.
      Guaranteed returns, pressure to deposit, fake regulatory claims, withdrawal-unlock fees, requests for secret keys or one-time codes.
    11. Why is journaling useful?
      It helps identify whether rules were followed and reveals performance patterns over a meaningful sample.
    12. What is the safest beginner milestone?
      Understanding the instrument and costs, controlling risk, and following a tested process — not hitting a profit target.
    Closing

    Final principles to remember

    The short versionThere is no guaranteed method for making money in financial markets. Every sentence below is a variation on one idea: protect the process, and the process protects the capital.
    1. Understand the product before trading it; products with similar charts can have very different legal and financial risks.
    2. Know your costs, including spread, commission, financing, slippage, and taxes where applicable.
    3. Decide where the trade idea is invalid before entry, then size the position around the risk.
    4. Leverage is optional; learning without leverage is often more sensible for beginners.
    5. A high win rate alone does not prove profitability.
    6. Evaluate average wins, average losses, costs, drawdown, and rule adherence.
    7. Treat demo trading as practice, not proof that live performance will match.
    8. Verify brokers, protect credentials, and avoid guaranteed-profit claims and paid recovery schemes.
    9. Keep records and change a strategy based on evidence, not anger, fear, or a few recent trades.
    10. If you cannot afford the loss, do not risk the money.

    Further learning — prefer primary and reputable sources

    • Your country's official financial regulator: verify firms, warnings, product permissions, investor-protection rules.
    • Official exchange education pages: contract specifications, trading hours, order types, market rules.
    • Central-bank and national statistics websites: policy statements and official economic releases.
    • Company investor-relations pages and regulatory filings: earnings, annual reports, material disclosures.
    • Reputable investor-education resources from public regulators and established exchanges.

    Always check publication dates and jurisdiction. Online videos, influencers, and forums can be useful for ideas, but verify factual claims against official documents and never treat a signal as a substitute for your own risk assessment.

    End of course · Keep learning, practise patiently, and put risk control before profit targets.
